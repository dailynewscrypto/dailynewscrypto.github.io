Stable or volatile pool? Match the curve to the tokens

Stable pools can cut trading costs for assets that should stay near the same price, while volatile pools suit tokens that may move apart. Choose by the pair.

Crypto Daybook NewsroomOct 31, 2026 · 21:22 UTC3 min read

Choose a stable pool for assets expected to stay near the same price, and a volatile pool for assets that may move apart. The curve sets how the pool’s token balances change as people trade, which affects the price they get and the fees liquidity providers earn.

A volatile pool usually follows the constant-product rule: the two token balances multiply to roughly the same value after each trade. As a trade removes one token and adds the other, the price shifts. Larger trades tend to cause more slippage, the price movement caused by a trade. How does a stable pool curve work?

A stable pool curve keeps prices steadier when the tokens are close to their intended relative value. It lets traders swap more with less price movement near that balance. Many stable-swap curves blend a constant-sum approach near balance with a constant-product approach when balances become uneven. This helps the pool keep trading even when one token is in greater demand.

That efficiency depends on the assets staying close in value. A pool might pair two versions of a dollar-pegged token, or a token with a redeemable version intended to track it. If one token loses its peg, traders may sell it into the pool and take out the token still worth more. The pool can then hold mostly the weaker asset.

When is a volatile pool the better fit?

A volatile pool is generally the better fit when there is no reason to expect the tokens to trade near a fixed ratio. A token paired with a major cryptocurrency, for example, can move sharply against it. The constant-product curve adjusts prices as trades change the balances, and it continues to quote trades across a wider range of prices.

That flexibility has a cost. Traders may face more price movement on each swap than in a well-balanced stable pool. Liquidity providers also face the risk that the pool’s mix changes as prices move. Their deposit can be worth less than simply holding the same tokens, especially after one token rises or falls sharply.

What should you check before choosing a pool?

Start with the expected price relationship between the tokens, then compare the pool’s terms and depth. A useful shortlist is:

  • Price relationship: Choose stable only when the assets are meant to track each other.
  • Pool balance: Check whether the tokens are near the intended ratio. A stable curve works best around its target.
  • Trade size: Compare the expected swap price and slippage for the amount you plan to trade.
  • Provider risk: Consider whether changing token prices or a broken peg could leave the pool weighted toward one asset.

For most readers, the pair’s likely price behavior is the deciding factor. Stable curves can make swaps more efficient for closely linked assets, but that advantage fades when their values diverge. When prices can move freely, a volatile pool is the more suitable curve.
