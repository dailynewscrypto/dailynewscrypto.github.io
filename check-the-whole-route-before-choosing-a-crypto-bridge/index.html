Check the Whole Route Before Choosing a Crypto Bridge

A bridge’s arrival estimate covers only part of a cross-chain transfer. Check exchange withdrawal time, source confirmations and destination steps before choosing.

Crypto Daybook NewsroomOct 17, 2026 · 07:40 UTC3 min read

When moving funds from an exchange through a bridge, judge the full route’s arrival time, not just the bridge’s estimate. Your transfer may wait for the exchange to send it, for the source network to confirm it, and for the bridge to deliver funds on the destination network. Each step can add time.

What does a bridge arrival time include?

A bridge’s estimate usually describes the bridge portion of a transfer, rather than every step from your exchange account to your destination wallet. First, the exchange must process your withdrawal and send it to the address you provide. Then the source network records the transaction. The bridge can act once it sees enough confirmations, which are checks that a transaction has been added to the network.

After that, the bridge may need to send the corresponding asset on the destination network. Some bridges use liquidity already available there; others rely on steps such as locking an asset on one network and issuing a version on another. The final transfer also needs to be recorded on the destination network before the funds show in your wallet.

For a Solana-to-BNB Chain route, the asset and network steps matter too. A fuller guide to using Biswap for a Solana-to-BNB Chain transfer lays out that example in more detail. The general lesson is to check which asset will arrive and on which network.

Why can two transfers take different times?

Arrival depends on every stage, and a slow stage can hold up the whole route. An exchange may take longer to process a withdrawal, while network congestion can make transactions wait or require more time before a bridge treats them as confirmed. The bridge may also have a processing window or need destination-side liquidity.

Estimates can differ because services measure different things. One may show the expected bridge processing time after deposit; another may include network confirmation. Treat any estimate as a guide, not a promise. If the service provides a status page or transaction tracker, use it to see which stage is still pending.

How should you compare bridge arrival estimates?

Compare the complete route and the asset you will receive. Before sending a larger amount, check these details:

  • How long the exchange says its withdrawal may take.
  • Which source network and token the bridge accepts.
  • Whether the estimate includes source confirmations and destination delivery.
  • Which token and network your wallet will receive, and whether you can use them there.

Also check the fees at each stage. A faster route may cost more, or may depend on liquidity that is not available for every asset or amount. Confirm the destination address and network before withdrawing; a transfer sent to the wrong network can be difficult or impossible to recover.

For most readers, the better choice is the route with a clear end-to-end estimate, a supported destination asset and visible progress at each step. A short bridge estimate alone does not tell you when funds will be ready to use.
